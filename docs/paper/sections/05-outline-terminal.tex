\section{The remaining periods and the main theorems}\label{sec:outline-terminal}

For periods with at most five pieces, the closing equations can be solved directly. Comparing
their costs with the octagon's completes the density bound. To classify the disks attaining
equality, the comparison must also identify their trajectories.

\subsection{The four- and five-piece comparisons}\label{sec:outline-short}

The closing equations leave four-piece periods with only forward switches and a two-parameter
family of five-piece periods. Entirely reverse five-piece patterns are already excluded by
\cref{thm:class-R}; all other patterns with at most five pieces fail the closing equations
(\cref{prop:terminal-words}).

The four-piece family contains the octagon. Its cost has a unique minimum at the symmetric
configuration, where the four pieces have equal durations. The proof locates this minimum by
monotonicity and an exact logarithmic inequality (\cref{thm:four-density}).

The five-piece family requires a strict comparison. It approaches the octagon when one piece
shrinks to zero duration, so no uniform positive gap is possible near that boundary. A concave
envelope gives the strict inequality throughout the family (\cref{thm:five-density}). The two
comparisons yield the following theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Terminal comparison]\label{thm:terminal}
Every normal vertex period with at most five pieces costs at least $\Aoct$.
\end{theorem}

Equality holds only for the octagon's four-piece motion, up to a cyclic relabeling of the
controls. \Cref{prop:terminal-equality} states the precise identification of its frame and state.
The comparisons and the equality statement are proved in \cref{sec:terminal}.

The cost comparison proves the lower bound. Identifying the minimizing trajectory then shows that
its disk is an affine image of the smoothed octagon.

\subsection{The lower bound}\label{sec:terminal-proofs}

\begin{theorem}[Cost of a minimizer]\label{thm:minimizer-cost}
Every global minimizer of the control problem costs at least $\Aoct$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $(T,u,g,X)$ be a global minimizer. The octagon's admissible trajectory has cost $\Aoct$
(\cref{prop:octagon-trajectory}), so the minimizer is competitive. By \cref{thm:finite-normal},
choosing a switch as the starting time gives a normal vertex period with the same cost and with
state in $\hstarstar$. It has at most five pieces by \cref{thm:at-most-five}. Its cost is
therefore at least $\Aoct$ by \cref{thm:terminal}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:control}]
A global minimizer exists by \cref{thm:equivalence}. Every admissible trajectory costs at least
as much as this minimizer, which costs at least $\Aoct$ by \cref{thm:minimizer-cost}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:main}\textup{(b)}]
Let $K_0$ be a worst disk. By \cref{thm:equivalence}, its realization is a global minimizer of
cost $\sqrt{12}\,\delta(K_0)$. Hence
\[
  \sqrt{12}\,\delta(K_0)\ge\Aoct=\sqrt{12}\,\doct.
\]
Every centrally symmetric convex disk $K$, translated to the origin if necessary, has density at
least $\delta(K_0)$. Translation preserves density, so $\delta(K)\ge\doct$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Recovering the disk in the equality case}\label{sec:outline-equality}

Let a disk attain the least packing density. Apply the finite-period reduction to its realization
as a global minimizer. The terminal equality statement identifies this trajectory with the
octagon's after a cyclic relabeling.

To recover the given disk, we reverse the choices made in the reduction. The cyclic relabeling
permutes the six contact curves, and the change of starting time changes their reference frame.
Reversing the initial linear normalization then carries the octagon's boundary onto the boundary
of the disk. \Cref{sec:equality-rigidity} gives the details and proves the following rigidity
statement.

\begin{theorem}[Rigidity]\label{thm:rigidity}
Every worst disk is the image of the octagon $\KH$ under an invertible linear map of the plane.
\end{theorem}

The converse follows from the lattice formulation of density. An invertible linear map
multiplies the disk area and the lattice cell area by the same factor. Translation changes
neither density.

\begin{proposition}[Affine invariance]\label{prop:converse}
Let $K$ be a convex disk symmetric about the origin, $A$ an invertible linear map of the plane and
$b$ a vector. Then $\delta(AK)=\delta(K)$ and $\delta(K+b)=\delta(K)$. In particular
$\delta(A\KH)=\doct$ for every invertible linear map $A$.
\end{proposition}

Finally, Hales's octagon $\KH$ is an invertible linear image of Reinhardt's $\Koct$
(\cref{prop:reinhardt-construction}). The two constructions therefore have exactly the same
affine images. The explicit map and the proof of affine invariance are in
\cref{sec:equality-construction,sec:equality-converse}.

\subsection{Completion of the main theorem}\label{sec:equality-proofs}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:main}\textup{(a)}]
The disk $\KH$ is centrally symmetric and convex, with greatest packing density $\doct$
(\cref{prop:octagon-trajectory}). The invertible linear map of
\cref{prop:reinhardt-construction} carries it onto $\Koct$. Central symmetry, convexity,
compactness and nonempty interior pass to this image, and \cref{prop:converse} preserves its
density.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:main}\textup{(c)}]
Suppose a centrally symmetric convex disk $K$ has density $\doct$, and let $c$ be its centre.
By part~(b), $K-c$ is a worst disk. Rigidity gives $K-c=L\KH$ for an invertible linear map $L$.
Thus $K$ is an affine image of $\KH$, and hence of $\Koct$ by
\cref{prop:reinhardt-construction}. Conversely, every invertible affine image of $\Koct$ has
density $\doct$ by part~(a) and \cref{prop:converse}.
\end{proof}
